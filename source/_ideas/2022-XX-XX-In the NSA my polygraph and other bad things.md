## What actually happened

This "one hour interview" turned into 8 hours of interviews on two days.  

The format was basically a few questions that somebody with an IQ of 60 or more should recognize as the correct answer being YES, and a few more with the correct answer of NO.

We went through the questions forwards, backwards, odd-numbered first, random order. And in-between she would ask me (sometimes with the wires on or off) if I understood the questions. And then she asked me if I stole anything. And I said I stole a bag of chips in freshman year.

**More crimes**

Hours/days later I also admitted to "crimes" of taking toilet paper from a hallway bathroom and writing my initials in wet contrete. I specifically picked those two because it is feasible that I could feel bad about it without it actually being a crime—I never said I wasn't *allowed* to take that toilet paper from my neighbors, I never said wasn't *permitted* by somebody to tag a concrete. Definitely overthinking it. And underthinking it.

Either way she didn't ask any follow up questions on those "crimes" so I felt like that was what was required of me to proceed.

**The room**

At the time I was in college. Waking up in Fort Meade at 6am was a first for me. And that was the first time I had coffee. Then imagine sitting in a chair all day staring at a cute redheaded woman from the FBI. And she is staring at you. And she is watching your heart rate, and you can hear it too (this was 2005, they had a physical meter back then). And your mission this whole time seems like to keep your heartrate down. Are there people that can actually do this?

Every man learns a skill in life where they can watch a woman walk across the room but the whole time keep their eyes strictly pointed somewhere else. On this interview day, I learned the exact opposite skill—looking at her and desperately seeing everything except her in the room.

My body is not built for sitting down and breathing consistently. The proctor frequently told me to "stop breathing like that", "stop moving" and "you're messing things up". When I told her my arm was going numb, she said if I take it off we'll have to start over. So if you add it up maybe I actually took dozens of polygraph tests in these days.

With the physicality of it, saying "yes" and "no" a few times on cue—while not breathing the way you normally do, resisting each time your body wants to move, not seeing the girl in front of you, and not letting your mind think of anything—can be quite hard.

**The thoughts**

A few times the proctor seemed pretty upset with me. She even showed me what she was looking at. "I can't use this, we need to start over and do it again." Maybe this is standard operating procedure, I don't know. She asked, when I was unplugged, what images these questions were bringing up in my mind.

One question was about drugs. And I shared a recent story about my friend that died and was definitely on my mind.

One question was about terrorist activity. And I shared how I felt about the 9/11 terrorist attacks. While you're saying "no" I don't plan to overthrow the government of the United States and whatever the full question was, there are definitely some violent images that came to mind.

Even with this she told me that I wasn't giving a clear enough response to the questions.
